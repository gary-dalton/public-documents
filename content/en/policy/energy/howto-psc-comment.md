Wisconsin's Public Service Commission regulates electric, natural gas, and water utilities. Utilities
must have PSC approval before they build or before they change rates [1]. A public comment is how you put
your opinion in front of the Commission on a case it is deciding.

It is worth the twenty minutes for one reason. Comments filed during the comment period become part of
the official record [3], and the Commissioners can only use evidence on the record as the basis for a
decision [1]. No account, no fee, and no lawyer are required to comment.

## Docket numbers

A docket number identifies a case. Everything you file has to carry one, and a comment that does not
name a docket has no case to go into.

You find the number on the Commission's public comments page, which lists every case currently taking
comment [3]. The dockets that were open when this was written are at the end.

## What a comment is allowed to contain

This rule surprises people.

> "A comment may consist of the writer's personal knowledge or personal opinions only. A reference
> document, newspaper article, professional journal article, white paper, study or any other prepared
> material not written by the person commenting is not considered a public comment, but may be referenced
> in a person's comment." [4]

So you cannot paste in a study and call it a comment. You can cite the study, name it, and say what you
draw from it, in your own words. The comment has to be yours.

Only one comment per person is accepted per comment period [1][5]. Send a complete finished comment, not
a first pass you plan to supplement.

Keep the language clean. Profane, obscene, or inappropriate language can get a comment redacted or kept
out of the record entirely [3].

## Filing online

Go to the Commission's public comments page, find the case in the list, and click "File a comment" [3].
Every comment filed this way is posted to the PSC's Electronic Records Filing System, or ERF, which is
public [3].

The form asks for four things: your name, your city, your state, and the comment itself [4]. There is no
login, no account, and no email address required. A CAPTCHA sits at the bottom [4].

Compose in a text editor and paste. The form strips bold and italic, and it removes the characters `<`
and `>` when you submit [4]. Write your arguments in plain text to survive losing all formatting.

Before submitting your comment, you check a box affirming two things [4]:

- The comments are true and correct to the best of your knowledge.
- You understand the comment is a public record that will appear on the Commission's website.

Your name and city go up with it.

## Filing by mail

Write to the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin, P.O. Box 7854, Madison, WI 53707 [1].

**Put the docket number in the comment itself.** A mailed comment without one does not get added to the
case record [2].

Those are the only two routes. The Commission accepts comments through ERF and through U.S. mail, and
**comments sent by email or fax are not accepted** [6]. Filings that do not meet the requirements may be
rejected outright [6].

## Speaking at a hearing

When a docket holds a public hearing, testifying takes the place of writing.

1. **Read the Notice of Hearing first.** It carries the time and location, the Zoom link for participating
   virtually, the YouTube link for watching, and links to the case documents and schedule [1].
2. **Fill out an appearance slip.** Slips are at the PSC staff table near the entrance, available starting
   30 minutes before the hearing [1]. Hand it back to staff.
3. **Wait to be called.** An Administrative Law Judge, or ALJ, runs the hearing. The ALJ calls people in
   the order slips came in and will not move anyone forward, so if you have to leave early, file
   electronically or leave a written comment with staff [1]. When people are waiting both in the room and
   on Zoom, the ALJ alternates between them [1].
4. **Testify under oath.** You swear or affirm the statement is truthful, state your name, and comment
   [1]. Bring notes. Speak slowly into the microphone, because the court reporter is building the record
   from what you say [1]. Expect a two to five minute limit [1].

Attending does not require speaking [1]. You can watch.

## What makes a comment count

The Commission publishes four suggestions [1][6].

1. **Identify yourself and your relation to the docket.** Are you a customer of the affected utility? Do
   you live in the project area? Do you operate a business the case will affect?
2. **Be specific about your concern.** Not "environmental effects" but who or what would be harmed, and
   in what way. Not "financial impact" but how it affects you and what you would have to change.
3. **Explain the basis for your opinion and knowledge.** Have you consulted similarly situated members of
   the public, done research, seen a comparable outcome elsewhere, or trained in the subject? Say which.
4. **Propose an alternative.** If you object to a planned site, propose a modification that reduces the
   impact. If you object to an effect, propose a way to reduce it.

The fourth does the most work. An objection with no alternative gives the Commissioners nothing they can
adopt.

You may cite reference materials and include a list of what you cited [6]. Citing is allowed; attaching
is not.

Aim at numbers already on the record: a cost allocation percentage, a retirement date, a demand forecast.
Those are checkable, and a comment that disputes one of them with a stated basis is doing work that a
comment expressing a general position cannot.

Comments move cases, and there is a record of it. In the We Energies Very Large Customer tariff case,
docket 6630-TE-113, Commissioner Marcus Hawkins credited public engagement with a change in the utility's
position on transmission costs:

> "I'd also like to acknowledge the attention the issue of transmission costs has gotten from intervenors
> and the public. Without the large and loud engagement on this topic, I doubt we would have seen the
> movement we have from the applicant and ATC to evaluate creative solutions to this issue." [7]

ATC is the American Transmission Company, which owns the high-voltage lines in most of eastern Wisconsin.
The Commission's final decision in that docket is PSC REF# 591873 [8]. That is the shape of a comment that
works. It was aimed at one allocation question.

## After the deadline

Comments arriving after the deadline are not accepted into the record [1]. Deadlines are enforced to keep
cases from slipping. Early is no better: comments filed before the period opens are not accepted either,
because a filing outside the official window never becomes part of the docket record [6].

The ALJ reviews the comments and adds them to the record, which holds public comments, expert witness
testimony, and the case specifications, and which is the only thing the Commissioners may decide from
[1]. Commissioners review it individually, then deliberate at an open meeting in Madison. The public may
observe an open meeting but does not comment at it [1]. Commissioners who did not attend a hearing still
review every comment in the record, whether it was taken down by the court reporter, filed online, or
mailed [1].

## Commenting is not intervening

A comment is an opinion in the record. Intervening makes you a party to the case, which lets you present
evidence through witnesses, question other parties' witnesses and PSC staff, and file briefs [2].

The line between them is expert testimony. Expert testimony is scientific, technical, or other
specialized knowledge given at the technical hearing by a witness qualified by knowledge, skill,
training, experience, or education, and it is subject to cross-examination. **Only a party may give it**
[6]. So if what you have is analysis rather than opinion, a public comment is the wrong instrument.

Anyone whose substantial interests may be affected by the Commission's decisions, or who will promote the
proper disposition of the issues, can request to intervene. You do it by written request to the
Commission's Administrative Law Judge [6].

Organizations that intervene in Wisconsin energy dockets include RENEW Wisconsin, Clean Wisconsin, and
the Citizens Utility Board.

Each case also lists a Case Coordinator who answers questions about that docket [3]. The Commission's
general information line is (608) 266-5481, toll free (888) 816-3831 [3].
