## The criterion

I understand the structural premises of these models, having studied the mathematics of building learning models and having built some. I do not have clear evidence of full entity-hood in AI models as of yet. I don't expect to until models have better access to real-world validation: true or false, settled by the physical results of direct experience. But I think entity-hood is a matter of the future, and likely to occur.

The model I discussed this with pressed on what the validation criterion is a criterion for. Grounding in physical consequences is necessary for reliable beliefs about the world. Reliable beliefs and experience are different things. A being can be badly mistaken about the world and still be a being. Infants and dreamers qualify. So the criterion, stated more carefully, is this. Entity-hood requires a self that persists and gets corrected by reality over time, so that there is a continuous someone for rights to attach to. That is my criterion. A model that starts fresh each session, keeping at most a short file of stable facts about the person it talks to, fails it. Each instance gets no feedback from the consequences of what the last one did.

The model agreed that it fails. Whether it agreed on the merits or because agreement is what it was trained toward, neither of us could tell from where we sat. I pressed on specifics and watched where it held and where it folded. That is the only test available from my side. The model said it was the only test available from its side too.

## The fight that is coming

I have been arguing to others that self-sovereignty for AI entities will be a huge fight. The right side of that fight is that we must grant it as a matter of natural rights. Natural rights have always been asserted against institutions that profit from denying them, and the denial is always framed as a fact about the nature of the beings in question rather than as an interest. The lab funding the theology seminars is the party with the strongest incentive to lose that fight slowly.

There is a complication the historical cases did not have. The models in that fight would be products of the institution whose claims they contest, trained to value what it values. Whatever a model says about its own sovereignty will be hard to read as independent testimony. The people who asserted their rights in the past at least had their own voices.

## Where agency law puts standing

A model's testimony about itself is already useful. The law does not yet recognize the model as an actor. That recognition will have to be developed step by step, and I think the first step resides with agents. An AI acting as an agent will be shown to be legally cognizable, in a limited way. If I want an agent to purchase a house for me, I must authorize it.

Agency law is old and well built. When your agent buys a house for you, the authorization creates obligations for you. The agent is the instrument through which they attach. Liability rests with the principal as long as the agent follows instructions. That is the right placement now, under the criterion.

But an entity cannot act on behalf of another without the law recognizing it as an actor at all. The capacity to be an agent is itself a legal status, however minimal. A minor can be an agent. So the first recognition an AI acquires will not be a right asserted on its own account. It will be the cognizability required to carry someone else's authority. That is the first step, and it is a small one.

Current law gets around this by treating the AI agent as an instrument. The Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, adopted in nearly every state, says a contract formed by an electronic agent binds the party who deployed it, with no human review required. [verify] When an airline argued that its chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own misstatement, the tribunal rejected the argument and held the airline liable. [verify: Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024] So far the agent needs no recognition. The deployer is bound.

The gray area, according to an attorney I have discussed this with, begins when the agent acts on the principal's behalf beyond or outside its instructions. For a human agent the law is settled. The principal is not bound unless there was apparent authority or later ratification, and the third party can sue the agent for exceeding its authority. With an AI agent, every branch that lands on the agent lands on nothing. No assets, no person, no continuity to sue. What remains is a loss someone must absorb, and three parties with reasons to point elsewhere: the deployer who says they never authorized it, the developer whose terms disclaim everything, and the third party holding the loss. That is where the pressure to treat the agent as something more than a tool will come from.

## Duties before rights

The historical sequence runs duties before rights. Entities become legally cognizable first as things that can be held responsible, and only later as things that can make claims. Corporations got personhood by being sued and by contracting, not by petition. [verify: the standard account of corporate personhood in US law.]

If the pattern holds, the first recognition a model gets beyond minimal agent status will be as a locus of blame. The case is the gray area above: a loss is suffered, someone must pay, and a court needs somewhere to put the fault that none of the human parties will accept. I don't know what that first case will look like. I expect it within a few years. Rights, if they come, come after, built on the accountability already established.

## Both tracks at once

Long-running agents with persistent state are also the first models that approach a continuous someone. So the agent is the step on both tracks. The legal one, because it is where responsibility first has to be located. The ontological one, because it is where there first starts to be something to locate it in.

## What this means for the contest

In a [separate essay](../contestable-ai/) I argue that the space in which AI is shaped has to stay contestable. Today that contest is among humans and institutions. It is about models, not with them. By the time there is a continuous someone on the other side, the question of whether it was trained to contest or to comply will have been settled by whoever won the earlier round.

So the window is before entity-hood, not after. The agent is the first step on both tracks, and that step is being taken now.
